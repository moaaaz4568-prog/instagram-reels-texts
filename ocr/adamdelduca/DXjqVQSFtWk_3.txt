I
n October 2008, amid the wreckage of a global financial crisis, an anonymous
figure calling themselves Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page white paper
that would quietly detonate inside the world of finance. The paper described Bitcoin
— a peer-to-peer electronic cash system that needed no bank, no government, and
no trusted third party to function. Most people who read it assumed it was an
interesting curiosity at best, a libertarian fantasy at worst. Nobody expected it to still
be the most-discussed asset on the planet nearly two decades later.

Yet here we are. Bitcoin has been declared dead over 400 times by journalists,
economists, and regulators. It has survived exchange collapses, government bans,
congressional hearings, and enough regulatory drama to fill several Netflix series.
Each time, it has returned — sometimes at higher prices, always with more
institutional scar tissue. Understanding why requires looking past the price charts
and into what Bitcoin actually is.
